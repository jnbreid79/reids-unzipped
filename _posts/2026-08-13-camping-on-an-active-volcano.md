## Arriving in Port Resolution

After leaving New Caledonia we checked into Vanuatu at Port Resolution, on the island of Tanna. We had hoped to check into Aneityum, the Southernmost island in Vanuatu, however the officials there are either not currently processing the formalities for yachts, or were away elsewhere in Vanuatu at the time. So Port Resolution it was. The officials here (customs, immigration and biosecurity) drive (or rather, are driven, a service for which we split the cost between cruisers) from the town of Lenakel on the other side of the island. We did see customs on the first day, after which we were free to land and explore, but everything here works on island time (and strictly cash only)! We would wait until the next day for immigration and biosecurity.

We woke up on our first morning surrounded by local fishing boats, canoes carved out with a single outrigger. We were in high demand immediately, charging one fisherman's iPhone which he returned to collect in the afternoon. The boats spread out and work together, as soon as there is any sign of fish great activity starts up with lots of shouting, water slapping (with paddles) and paddling into position to lay out the nets. We didn't see any evidence of great fish hauls being landed (or even a single fish) but they must have caught something since we heard later it hadn't been too bad a day.

![Immaculate village road]({{ site.baseurl }}/assets/images/gallery/2026-08/immaculate-village-road.webp)

The villagers have designated roles, some are fisherman, some are gardners (farmers), some builders etc. etc. We didn't work out how you are assigned your trade, however everything is shared, there are no shops without embarking on the three hour bumpy drive across the island, and very few people can drive. Vanuatuan's take pride in the presentation of their homes, gardens and villages. Houses are mostly just corrugated iron sheets standing up as walls, fabric doors and coconut palm thatched roofs. The 'roads' are swept daily to keep smooth, clean of leaves and look immaculate, and there are several communual areas (nakamal) under banyan trees where the men meet in the afternoons to discuss all community matters and drink kava.

There are several village communities in and around Port Resolution, many of which compete for business in taking customers up their sacred volcano, Mt. Yasur. We arranged to do the tour with Donovan, in a group that rapidly expanded to all five of the family boats we were with; three of us having sailed from New Caledonia at the same time (Zipper, Emotion and Jadamama), and a further two arriving shortly after from Fiji (Freeranger and June).


## Climbing Mt. Yasur

We met on the black sand beach at the head of the bay. Much of this is a hot spring, which runs all the way around the western side of the anchorage. At anchor we have been watching various rocks and areas of the forested banks periodically producing steaming clouds from the geothermal activity beneath and here we could walk on the hot, but not too hot, sand.

We hauled our overnight kit up to the village for a pick-up to drive it all up the hill and we set off on foot. We were nearly outnumbered by the locals as lots of the kids get out of school to come and practice their English on us foreigners. This is particularly encouraged when English-speaking children are visiting as they all play together, and in that regard ours was a popular group!

We had a few stops on the way whilst Donovan taught us about the local banyan trees (you could spend all day looking at the amazing banyan trees on the walk up!) and how they are held as a model of leadership in the community. We stopped to pick up vegetables for our supper, mainly cassava and taro with spring onions. The walk was hot work (luckily not too hot a day) so we also stopped for some fresh coconuts. On our travels we've struggled to get even a few feet off the ground to reach tempting looking coconuts, but we've never seen anything like this - a handful of the kids climbed enourmously high coconut trees as though they were shinning up a ladder - no ropes, no cut outs, just a 50ft vertical trunk. They jumped on the coconuts to knock them down, many landing amongst their friends down on the ground, fortunately none landing actually _on_ one but were certainly close - health and safety haven't visited here yet!

## The campsite

As we got closer to the top we started to hear the rumblings and eruptions of the volcano - a sign it is 'happy' according to Chucky (one of our guides). Finally the plains opened up into a massive expanse of black sand (which was actually volcanic ash). It was nice being up there on an overcast day without much wind; we'd heard it gets very cold at night. However without the wind we found we were subject to the ash falling instead, and wow does it get everywhere! Tents went up but keeping the ash out even as you opened doors carefully and quickly was a fruitless exercise. Everything was covered. Our scalps were gritty like a day of rolling around on a sandy beach. It was made worse by the occasional drizzle that made everything stick, at times it was hard to distinguish between rain and ash.

## Looking into the erupting crater

By the time we had our tents erected it was dark and we could see the last of the other tourists (presumably driving over from Lenakel on the other side of the island) walking down the ridge by torch light. The crater's red glow looming over us was majestic, pulsing brighter with the now very loud eruptions as we were so close. At the top were some railings (which you definitely didn't want to risk leaning very hard against, the risk/reward is not in your favour as the volcano will win that everytime). The volcano erupted every few minutes, and each seemed to be preceded by the sound of waves swooshing around in the depths below. Then we'd see the glow before explosions of molten rock flew up from below. Finally we'd hear the accompanying boom. The scale of each varied hugely, and I think the first truly loud explosion we each experienced will probably last with us forever. I know Yasur has been continuously erupting with these 'small' events for centuries, but that doesn't stop you wondering if this astronomic-sounding explosion means it's had enough and decided to move on geologically, or whether it is indeed just another one of the millions that preceded it. Fortunately, of course, it was the latter. But the guttural explosion that makes everything shake and even presses your trousers against your legs as the shock wave passes does give you the feeling it is something more serious.

> Is this the end, or is that explosion 'normal'?!

![An eruption caught on camera]({{ site.baseurl }}/assets/images/gallery/2026-08/volcanic-explosion.webp)
![Silhouette, looking into the volcano during an eruption (credit: Pete, June)]({{ site.baseurl }}/assets/images/gallery/2026-08/silhouette-at-night.webp)

We stayed watching for about an hour in absolute awe of the power of the volcano. However it wasn't for everyone, understandably some of us were pretty scared and wanted nothing more than to be as far away from this erupting volcano as possible. Some opted to join those who'd already planned on a more comfortable night back on their respective boats and after a long walk in the dark back to the entrance, they got a bumpy ride back to the beach for welcome showers and comfy beds! The rest of us headed back to camp and tucked into the Tanna soup that had been boiling up whilst we were on the top. We huddled under a tarp to try and escape the worst of the ash, but all succumbed to our tents long before 9pm once a sufficient number of marshmallows had been toasted.

## Sleeping (or not) on the volcano

It turns out the volcano doesn't go to sleep. Lying on the ground as it continued to erupt every few minutes through the night was intense! There were periods with longer gaps between eruptions, but they only led to a larger one to awaken you if you'd just started to drift off. Other than some of the children I don't think any of us slept more than a few minutes. Personally, between the cramp (I kept getting it in both legs at the same time), the hard ground, the noise and vibrations, wondering whether it would ever be possible to be ash-free again as my face was exfoliated on the jumper-for-a-pillow by the constant supply of ash/sand from my head, and the sheer adrenaline of it all, it was pretty easy to get up well before the 4am alarm went off for having not slept at all. It was still pitch black, but a massive relief as it was immediately apparent that the ash was no longer falling - there was some wind, the rain/drizzle had stopped, and the air was clear. Phew!

## Dawn

We headed back up to the top to once again see it in the dark and watch the dawn arrive. It was almost as majestic in the light, watching the ash clouds appearing, circling and dissipating was something that had been hard to appreciate in the night.  

> We did wonder about how our kids might feel if they woke up before we got back, realising they are still on the side of an active volcano, covered in ash, and have apparently been abandoned by their parents.

Boat kids learn to be pretty resilient, but would this be pushing it a bit far?! Fortunately not, not much phases them these days, certainly with Angus I had the feeling he'd be happier if I'd been away for longer!

The walk back down felt like a long one to me. One thing boat life doesn't do very well is keep you fit for long hikes, well not since we left New Zealand anyway. The highlight on the way down was a banyan tree swing. New roots shoot downwards from branches high up in the canopy. How strong they are is anyone's guess, and the consequences of not strong enough would be pretty severe with the drop below. With the local kids showing us all how it's done, many of our group had a go, including lots of the braver parents - hats off!

![Banyon tree swing]({{ site.baseurl }}/assets/images/gallery/2026-08/banyon-tree-swing.webp)

It's hard to describe how amazing an experience it was - we were all blown away by the epic force of nature that we witnessed. I remember hearing about it when Archie, Jo, Jemima and Euan came here on Mystic back in 2012, and finally I've managed to see for myself what all the fuss was about! Worth the wait, and I suspect Port Resolution has changed little, if at all, in the years since.
