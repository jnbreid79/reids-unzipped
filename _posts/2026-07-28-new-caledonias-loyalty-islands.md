The Loyalty Islands are a remote collection of islands lying 60 miles off the East coast of New Caledonia. They are made up of three main land masses; two uplifted coral islands (Mare and Lifou) and an atoll (Ouvea). The population is mainly Kanak (local indigenous people) speaking different languages on the different islands. These are places far removed from the French, bustling, modern cosmopolitan city of Noumea.

Land is owned locally by appointed chiefs, from whom permission needs to be sought in order to visit. You must visit the chief upon arrival do la coutume (this is a traditional custom ceremony) by bringing a gift, and in some cases a little money (in reality a small anchoring fee), to pay your respects and ask for consent to visit their land.

## Mare

We set sail on an overnight passage from Prony to Mare, timing the Havannah pass with the high tide slack and the beginning of the ebb (~1930). This meant an early supper and departure from Anse Majic just after dark at 1800. The channel is well lit and easy to navigate, although the 20 knot South Easterly meant a bumpy upwind start! Not wanting to arrive too early (the distance is a short overnight for Zipper) we set off with three reefs as we often seem to do. The inner pass was not eventful, thinking it might be rough we were pleasantly surprised that we must have timed it perfectly. However the outer pass, which on the chart doesn't even really look like a pass (it's plenty deep enough over a sunken reef on either side) was particularly lively - we got hit twice by big waves, and were relieved to finally bear away onto our beam reach for Mare. We then had a beautiful night sail, arriving after sunrise with plenty of light to pick our anchor spot in a nice big sandy patch in the Baie du Nord.

Baie du Nord is stunning, surrounded by cliffs on all sides and lush green trees. The water is beautifully clear with lots of coral bommies, as well as sandy patches to anchor in.  Our friends on Jadamama arrived shortly after us, so we joined forces and took their dinghy ashore to go and find the chief of the local village.

We had spotted a very steep ramp where a previous cruiser had suggested you could tie the dinghy up; on arrival at said ramp we realised it was far too steep to try to land the dinghy on, as well as being surrounded by coral, so the best option was to land everyone on the ramp and tie on to metal rings that had been put in the rocks, with a stern anchor to keep the dinghy in place. With all crew safely ashore and the dinghy secured we headed off to find the village. It was a beautiful walk along a lush green road with lots of big butterflies fluttering around. We were greeted at the first house we came to by a very welcoming man and his daughter. It turned out that he was the chief (Charles), so we made our coutume and he welcomed us very warmly, telling us about the big market going on further in town and inviting us back for tea at his house later.

We continued on and after 10 minutes or so came upon the (relatively small) market and were met by Robert who was selling brochettes (chicken kebabs) and took it upon himself to look after us and show us around. The market was a wonderful local affair where we stocked up on the limited fruit and veg in between the rounds of bingo that we have discovered are an essential part of local markets in the Loyalties. You can only buy things in between rounds as the ladies are far too absorbed in the game to trade while playing. We also had lunch at the 'restaurant' which was a choice of chicken, fish or egg, accompanied by the most delicious salads. After a wonderful couple of hours ashore with the locals, we returned to the boats and whilst the children played off Jadamama, we headed for a snorkel.

![Angus making friends through thumb wars]({{ site.baseurl }}/assets/images/gallery/2026-06/making-friends-outside-the-local-market.webp)

The coral in Baie du Nord was astonishingly good and varied and there were lots of caves and passages to swim between. Plenty of small fish around and to our delight we spotted an octopus, but no big fish. As we got out of the water at the end of day, Jamie spotted a manta ray just off our boat, an opportunity we couldn't miss, so we slipped back in and were graced with the most chilled out and curious manta swimming straight at us. It didn't hang around for long, but it was a privilege as always to spend time with these incredible creatures. We spent the following morning doing a bit more snorkelling and then headed to a different bay hoping for bigger fish and maybe more mantas?

The holding in the next bay was questionable at best, but we managed to get a semi decent hold. Kotare, another kid boat we made friends with in NZ also showed up and the following day we trekked through the forest to the other side of Mare. It was a fascinating walk and we came across an abandoned house and an old copra site (where they harvest and process the coconuts, maybe the two were linked?) before coming out onto the beach. It would have been beautiful were it not absolutely littered with plastic, it was such sad sight and so disappointing to see; we have never seen so much plastic on a beach and we couldn't quite work out how it had all ended up there, or where it's come from. But, there was also an abundance of nautilus shells and we found more than enough for everyone to take one home. After a night in this anchorage, we all decided to head back to the other side, which had been great holding and perfectly flat. It was now slightly rolly, which we couldn't quite work out, but still stunningly beautiful. So after another night we decided to head on to the next island in the chain - Lifou.

## Lifou

We left Baie du Nord at 3am and headed to Druelu, Lifou which ended with a windy upwind sail into the anchorage, but with the end in sight we were all alright with it. Anchor down in time for lunch and then it was time to go and find the chief and do la coutume.

Druelu is another beautiful anchorage with a sandy beach landing next to a ramp and the village immediately on the shore. At the top of the ramp we found a board informing us of the expected process for visiting yachts, it's not something we've come across before, but it made it clear what the expectations were and even showed the area we could anchor in. Having read the information we set off to find Pierre (who was not the chief but was in charge of anchoring fees) to pay our fee, he was away but we met his sister-in-law who told us to come back tomorrow morning to see him and in the meantime she showed us the building where the weddings took place and talked a bit about the village. We also got to see some of the traditional roundhouses they sleep in. We then set off to find the chief, who was also not home, but we met his wife Lilian, who was wonderful, and as it happens,  she carries out all the transactions and conversations anyway.

The following morning was market day in We, the town on the other side of the island - a 20 minute drive away. With no other option we decided to try our luck hitching a lift. We set off at 7.30 with Jadamama, with nine of us trying to hitch a lift, we decided that we'd just have to pile as many people in to whatever vehicles stopped for us. Slightly concerned about how long the adventure of hitch hiking whilst walking along the side of the road would entertain the children, we set off. We got incredibly lucky…the first car that passed us stopped, and it turned out to be a pick up truck, much to the children's delight. We all piled into the back and headed to We, the parents thankful for such incredible fortune, the kids thinking this was how it always worked!

There was a slight miscommunication and we ended up being dropped off at the supermarket rather than the market, but we managed to find the market in the end. It was definitely bigger than the Mare market we'd been to, but we still had to wait for the rounds of bingo to be completed! Fresh fruit and veg secured again, we headed back to the boat. It took slightly longer to hitch a lift home, but we were dropped right to the ramp which made life much easier with our shopping. We also managed to find Pierre and had a good conversation with him about the football world cup. Football is most definitely a universal language!

Knowing we were going to be here for a couple of days, we got the mini cat out for the children and did some sailing. The children sailed to a beach for a day out, it was a tough upwind leg coming home, but they were having a ball and living their best Swallows and Amazons life.

## Ouvea

After five nights in Druelu we sailed the 50Nm to Ouvea. It was a shifty passage with no wind, lots of wind, reaching, dead up wind, sunshine, rain, a bit of everything really. We were pleased to make it into the atoll, and catch a fish on the way through! Ouvea was gorgeous, exactly what we expected from an atoll, and much more. The white sandy beach in the south corner of the island stretched for miles and the palm trees dotted the shoreline. Anchoring in a big sandy patch was easy and then we headed into the village to find the chief, who was out, but we were told he would be home tomorrow; it seems to be a recurring theme.

We returned the next day and were able to do la coutume with the chief who gave us permission to snorkel in the passes and to go to the outlying islands in the south. We had really hoped we might be able to snorkel in the pass but had read mixed reports on whether this was allowed, but the chief could not have been friendlier and made it clear we were welcome.

With this in mind we put together a dinghy trip with Emotion, Jadamama and Kotare and headed for the main pass. The snorkelling surpassed our expectations and was some of the best we have had; there were lots of big, healthy, colourful corals and plenty of reef fish. We found lots of different passes with coral, reef sharks, fish and even a big eagle ray (and Liz swears she saw a manta ray!). With everyone beginning to get chilly we headed to an uninhabited island for a bonfire and marshmallows, before making the rough two mile trip upwind back to the boats. We've been having trouble with our outboard stalling from what seems to be a fuel feed issue, but as long as we continually pump the fuel bulb it seems to keep running - so, keep pumping, keep calm, and carry on...!

The following day we all had a delicious lunch at Olivia's restaurant on the beach. There are three shacks along the beach front of the village and each is a restaurant. Olivia's was recommended to us by fellow cruisers, but feedback from those who went to the others suggest they are also good. We had a fabulous meal of either chicken or fish with rice, yam and a delicious green papaya salad (nuggets and fries for the children). It happened to be Matariki (Maori New Year) and we were able to arrange a bonfire on the beach for that evening with everyone contributing various dishes; Maori fry bread from Kotare was a big hit! Dave from Kotare is an excellent musician and we were able to have some songs around the fire and shared the evening with Olivia and her family who were delightful. Jamie even got himself a Kanaky shirt.

After a great night in Mouli we headed north to explore further as a group of four kid boats. After a brief stop in Fayaoue, the main town in Ouvea, to pick up some provisions, we headed to Heo (St Joseph) in the north. Anchoring here was a bit precarious with a thin layer of sand over rock, but we managed to get a decent enough hold. We went ashore to do la coutume but due to some confusion we made la coutume with someone who wasn't actually the chief (Frankie, the guy who met us on the beach with a machete, so we did rather feel like doing what he suggested). He was outright hostile to start with making it clear we were not welcome, but after some perserverence in our fragile French we slowly brought him round to allowing us to stay. We think the turning point was when he realised he was going to get something out of us and that we were here to make la coutume. Upon speaking to a lovely lady called Marcelle in the church and then walking to see the Grande Chief (who it turns out doesn't have any involvement with the bay or running of Heo), we were told we had done the right thing trying to do la coutume and we were indeed welcome to stay in the north. We found out that the real chief had recently died, and his replacement is old and unwell, so there really was no clear procedure when we arrived. This guy calling himself Frankie was clearly making the most of that for himself!

We then rushed over to find a better anchorage for the night, much later in the day than planned due to the coutume debacle ashore. With the sun quickly dropping in the sky and clouds beginning to form overhead we tried to navigate the shallow waters littered with bommies. When the two leading boats nearly hit bommies due to the poor visibility, we all abruptly put down our anchors and got a good hold for the night.

In the morning it was a beach day for the kids and tag team snorkelling in the north pass for the adults. The girls made a hermit crab castle and the boys buried themselves up to their necks and all had an absolute blast. As is the way with these things, the wind got up and the second team were happy not to battle out into the pass, but to explore a more sheltered inlet instead.

On the way in, two fishermen waved us over; thinking they were going to tell us we couldn't be there, we headed over, only to discover they wanted to know what the weather forecast was and whether they should stay out to fish or return to the village. They also told us the other side of the inlet was tabu and we were not allowed there. This is after a hostage situation in 1988 where many people lost their lives on the island.

The weather then turned on us and we had to return to the south without having the time we were hoping for to properly explore the north. On our return, we anchored near Mouli bridge, enjoying more beach sports and another bonfire and marshmallows. Much to everyone's disappointment, it was then time to take the weather window back to Noumea. It wasn't a perfect window, still a bit upwind and not very long, but enough to get back to Grande Terre, and the seas were small.

> There is never enough time to see everything.

We encountered so many wonderful people in The Loyalty Islands; they have been overwhelmingly welcoming and friendly (other than "Frankie"!). Since Covid and the riots, many tourists have not returned and they were delighted to see cruising yachts again after many years without them. We loved our time in these outer islands of New Caledonia and were sad not to have longer to explore. It was hard to sail back to the main island knowing there was so much more to see and do, but that is always the way, there is never enough time to see everything and the weather dictates where we go and when.
