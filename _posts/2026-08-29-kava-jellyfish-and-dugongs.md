Although they are present in New Caledonia, we only had a fleeting encounter with a dugong in one of our anchorages there. So, on hearing that there a few hotspots for dugong in Vanuatu we didn't want to miss out.

Our passage north from Efate, after sadly saying goodbye to Paul, took us to the Maskelynes - a group of islands just off the south east corner of Malekula. We arrived into a beautiful anchorage where some of our friends were already anchored up having arrived a couple of days ahead of us. The village is run by Chief Phillip and his family, the Phillip family, who run guided tours to see and swim with the dugongs. They are incredibly knowledgeable and, we gather, run a fantastic tour. We joined everyone ashore for a wonderful potluck-style dinner largely provided by the family, and enjoyed our first ever kava welcome to the village. We relatively easily decided that we weren't going to seek out kava as often as we could in the future, but we did agree that it was a real privilege to be invited to participate in such a special and culturally significant custom. The taste of eating earthy dirt, followed by a numbness in the mouth and throat more associated with a trip to the dentist, isn't for everyone.

Unfortunately Sophie had badly sprained her ankle a couple of weeks earlier and it was taking a while to heal, so she was not yet back to swimming let alone with fins in relatively rough water. We therefore decided not to stay and do the tour separately ourselves, but to continue on to other nearby anchorages with our friends where we would have the chance to seek out the dugongs ourselves. 

Our next day was spent in a spectacular anchorage behind a low-lying sand island that disappears almost entirely at high tide. We set off in our dinghies to snorkel the outside of this reef, and almost immediately saw a dugong with calf swimming away from us. This was quickly followed by jellyfish stings, first Lily shortly followed by me. We all quickly exited the water and sought refuge back on our yachts. My sting was across my forehead, around me left ear, and across my chin (basically all around three sides of my mask). I didn't see anything, it must have been a very thin tentacle, but goodness yes it was painful. The pain did mostly start to go away under hot fresh water, however around my ear was just getting worse and worse. With some quick research Sophie covered it in flour and then scraped it off with a credit card. The theory is that any sting still on the skin sticks to the flour; a few mins later it definitely started improving - note for future: flour, credit card and hot water are the cure. After about an hour it was beginning to get more comfortable, and after a couple of hours I was feeling more like doing things again. Over the next couple of days the welts came up, and I had swelling and some itching (not bad, more annoying than anything) for at least five days afterwards. 

> Jellyfish sting - flour, credit card and hot water!

![Sandbar anchorage]({{ site.baseurl }}/assets/images/gallery/2026-08/sandbar-anchorage.webp)

More exploration in the dinghy around that island didn't reveal any more dugongs, but I did find a tasty looking lobster. I wasn't brave enough to go after it with my bare hands though, so we decided to move on. We found a beautiful little beach where a neighbouring island do a lot of their gardening (growing local crops) and traded 5 litres of petrol for some local produce. Swimming around the boat in the afternoon, waiting for our trading partner/farmer to return, Angus was stung by a jellyfish so again we got out the flour and card. We'd been a bit worried about not getting Angus back in the water again if he did get stung, but in reality he dealt with it very well and was back in in no time. 

![Beach by the fruit and vegetable gardens]({{ site.baseurl }}/assets/images/gallery/2026-08/beach-by-the-fruit-and-vegetable-gardens.webp)

## In search of the dugongs

So with Sophie and Angus both out of the water, and Isla wanting some quiet time, I took off in the late afternoon in search of a dugong further afield. I can certainly see the value of using the locals knowledge on a guided tour to find the dugongs and to properly learn about them. I wouldn't say I found any particular location where lots of them hang out, but I did find a couple of spots where they do swim past - mostly in pairs (assume mother/calf) but occassionally on their own too. Enjoying just hanging out with the fish (and a majestic eagle ray drifing past) was cool anyway, but I was patient and just quietly waiting my time was rewarded several times by passing dugongs. What a sight, they were not bothered by me at all, in fact many of them swam very close by or under me. What extraordinary creatures they are, and with the current flowing as it was I really got a sense of the enormous power in their tails - you certainly don't want to get too close behind one for risk of being hit. Their tail fin didn't need to touch the ground for a substantial ring of sand to be thrown outwards by its downstroke. Their skins were very scratched, but rather than from being attacked we think this is likely from rubbing against the coral, similar to how elephants rub against trees to get rid of an itch. Also they harbour any number of ramora/sucker fish, quite big ones. One ramora briefly broke away to come and check me out to see if I'd be a better long term ride; it only took a couple of seconds to decide no, it was far better off sharing a dugong with the others!

## Gaspard Bay

We spent the night anchored up in Gaspard Bay, a magical inlet sheltered from the prevailing wind and waves surrounded by mangroves and forest-clad hills. The sound of the forest was wonderful, something we'd not really heard much since the the Marlborough Sounds on New Zealand's south island. We realised the large birds flying high above us in the evening were in fact flying foxes - absolutely massive, more reminiscent of something from batman than the tiny bats we're used to back home in the UK.
