## Our route

Our 5 short weeks in Vanuatu took us from Port Resolution in the south to Sola in the north, visiting the islands of Tanna, Erromango, Efate (Port Vila), the Maskelynes, Ambrym, Malekula, Tutuba, Santo, Gaua and Vanua Lava. There are so many others we would have loved to visit; Aneityum, Epi, Pentecost, Ambae and Maewo to name just a few of the more well known. We can see how it's possible to spend a whole season here and still come back for the next.

![Zipper's track through Vanuatu]({{ site.baseurl }}/assets/images/gallery/2026-09/vanuatu-route.webp)

Through discussions with other cruisers before arriving in Vanuatu we were led to expect a very transactional experience, where the locals would be trying to extract as much as they can from us at every opportunity. We were therefore wary and apprehensive of what we might find. However, our experience sailing through the country, from Tanna in the south to Sola in the north, couldn't be further removed from this. We met the most welcoming and friendly people in all of our cruising to-date. We suspect this is because we did not do many organised 'tours', on which it seems there is a desire to 'upsell' resulting in the overall cost exceeding cruisers' initial expectations. We went to some places where there was a fee to visit, such as the blue holes on Santo, however knowing this in advance we were simply prepared for it and expecting it. In all other regards we met locals who were welcoming, happy to see us, spend time to talk and maybe make some small trades for fruit and vegetables.

## Vanutu's towns, villages and villagers

In Vanuatu we have visited villages and met people who are the poorest and have the least in comparison to everywhere else we have travelled. However our overriding memory is of generous and welcoming people and smiling children. The more remote the village and the poorer the community, the greater the welcome we experienced and the more generous the people. Everywhere we went we met children delighted to see us and keen to interact and play with us, their beaming smiles an absolute joy.

> The more remote the village and the poorer the community, the greater the welcome we experienced and the more generous the people

![Immaculate village 'street' in Port Resolution]({{ site.baseurl }}/assets/images/gallery/2026-08/immaculate-village-road.webp)
![Introducing the local kids in Bushman's bay to cricket]({{ site.baseurl }}/assets/images/gallery/2026-09/cricket-with-the-local-kids.webp)

We witnessed substantial disparity across the islands. Port Vila is by far the most developed, it's a relatively big town with concrete roads, pavements, solid buildings with integrated roofs and glass windows, doors etc. It has banks, chemists, health centres and supermarkets amongst the more local (and excellent) fruit and vegetable market and craft market. You wouldn't quite mistake a street here for one in a Western city, but it's getting that way. In contrast a 'street' in Port Resolution is a beautifully cleanly swept dust path between hedges separating plots of houses, which consist of coconut thatch roofs and panels of wall thatch or corrugated iron, with hanging cloths for doors. No glass, nor indeed any building materials to speak of here. Occasionally a building may have a concrete base, but mostly not. Equally a small hamlet we visited outside Bushman's bay in Gaua was even smaller with no road or street at all, simply a small collection of houses around a communal cleared grassy area with a volleyball court marked out. However everything there is again beautifully clean, small hedges and flowers growing up separating one plot from another, and not a scrap of rubbish to be seen. This is in stark contrast to Craig's Cove, on Ambrym, which was sadly completely overrun with rubbish. The path up from the beach and everywhere in the village was simply covered in rubbish, you were walking on it everywhere. I think the reasons for this are twofold:

1. In part this is a reflection of the remoteness of some villages, they simply don't have access to the consumables that generate rubbish. There are few, if any, supply ships, so they simply don't have any materials. They have no shop, they live subsistence lives where they only eat what they grow and catch themselves. Cash has limited value in these communities as there's genuinely nowhere to spend it, hence their desire to trade directly for the things they want - fishing gear, clothes, footballs, school books and stationery were the items we were asked for most.
2. Local decisions. Different villages take different approaches to disposing of the rubbish they do have. Some choose to burn it, which although good for the cleanliness of the village is possibly not so good for the environment and earth's atmosphere more generally. Some bury it. Others collect it into designated areas, presumably with a longer term hope of disposing in bulk, however despite best intentions this often simply results in wind and animals redistributing it, everywhere, as they overflow.

## Vanuatu Culture

From dancing to water music and fire shows to sand drawing we were fortunate enough to witness several local customs (kastom in Vanuatu, see [Vanuatu Culture: Traditional Kastom and Modern Tourism]({{ site.baseurl }}{% post_url 2026-09-25-vanuatu-culture-traditional-kastom-and-modern-tourism %})). The water music in Gaua really was a unique and extraordinary experience, I can't think of any other performances I've seen of any kind that are made with no props or materials at all, just the artists' bare hands and the ocean. It was mesmerising!

> No props or materials at all, just the artists' bare hands and the ocean

## Farming

Most villages keep at least some animals - lots of dogs and puppies (some wild but mostly pets), chickens seem to run wild everywhere (we managed to buy high quality eggs everywhere through Vanuatu), pigs are common and reared for eating (in particular for celebrations such as weddings, funerals and other festivals including annual circumcision events). Cattle are also quite common, some villages just having a handful and others with dozens or more.

With so many animals living around the villages their gardens (small local allotments, farm plots, where fruits and vegetables are grown) tend to be several miles outside the villages themselves. Often they are long walks uphill to more fertile soils higher up, mostly on the sides of volcanoes (not sure there are any hills here that are, or weren't, volcanoes). This means a long, daily, walk for the gardeners, however this is simply part of their lives, not dissimilar to commuting albeit out in the open taking exercise.

The advantage is that Vanuatu's rich and fertile volcanic soils, combined with warm climate and lots of rain, enable a lot of fruit and vegetables to be grown:

* Papaya and bananas are everywhere, these grow year-round, are prolific, and we love them.
* Tomatoes we also found everywhere, in particular cherry tomatoes. Having hardly seen a tomato between Panama and New Zealand this was particularly welcome.
* Similarly, we found green veggies we've not come across before in the islands - beans, snake beans and chouchou, as well as aubergine.
* More types of root vegetables are grown than I can remember. The most common ones we saw and used were yams, sweet potatoes, cassava and taro.
* The biggest surprise was grapefruit, not quite pamplemousse à la French Polynesia, but a close second for sure and this made us very happy. We also found water melon on occasion, still Angus' favourite.
* The biggest disappointment was that mango season hadn't started yet, and to make matters worse the season had already finished in the Solomon Islands when we arrived there so we missed out on both sides. Devastating (although in good news finishing this blog so late, we're now in PNG and I'm happy to report that mango season is firmly ON here!).

## Fishing

Between subsistence and commercial fishing we experienced all manner of local fishing techniques, as we wrote about in [Fishing Vanuatu-Style]({{ site.baseurl }}{% post_url 2026-09-02-fishing-vanuatu-style %}). ## Chinese influence

In Port-Olry on Santo there is an aspiration to expand the cattle-herd to a couple of thousand. Port-Olry is a much larger village, and with road connections to Luganville is, in comparison to small villages on the outer islands, well supplied. There is a tarmac road and a Chinese-funded desaliation plant currently being installed. We found a large Chinese influence across Vanuatu, in Port Vila and Luganville many of the shops are nondescript Chinese stores selling any random collection of crap, plus a few onions and perhaps eggs. No idea how you are supposed to tell which store sells what, why you might go to one rather than another, nor indeed why most of what they sell is of any interest to most people. This Chinese influence reminded us strongly of Vava'u in Tonga. From what we were able to gather, other than at government level where the deals are done, there is not much support for this Chinese involvement.

## Volcanoes

Vanuatu is a volcanic ridge of islands, part of the Pacific ring of fire, and seems to be particularly active at the moment. Many islands have one or more dangerously active volcanoes on them, and earthquakes are commonplace. This year seems to be particularly active globally; the volcano on Ambae erupted whilst we were further south in Port Vila (removing Ambae itself and the Eastward islands of Pentecost and Maewo from our potential route - not necessarily a problem for us as we were struggling to find other ways to decide which parts of Vanuatu to miss out) and Ambrym erupted as we left the north for the Solomon Islands only a couple of weeks after we were there. Sadly even these two events will have huge implications for the local populations, with water sources contaminated putting village life at risk. 

We were fortunately not directly impacted by an eruption, however it was often clear we were living under the influence of active volcanoes. There were places where we found ash accumulation on the boat, a fine gritty and dirty dust covering everything (and no doubt not doing a lot for the longevity of winches, clutches, motors, hinges and other sensitive marine equipment!), we saw the night-time orange glow of volcanic activity, all our stainless fittings have tarnished under the influence of low concentration sulphur emissions, and at times the background smell of a volcano was not overwhelming but nevertheless ever-present, and a relief to be free of on leaving.

[We will never forget our experience up on Mount Yasur]({{ site.baseurl }}{% post_url 2026-08-13-camping-on-an-active-volcano %}), peering into lava explosions powerful enough to generate visible shock waves. We also found natural hot springs in lots of places, as well as 'steaming' rocks and hillsides where the geothermal energy manages to find its outlets.

## Friends and adventures

We have been fortunate to share many of our experiences here in Vanuatu with friends, many of whom we met back in New Zealand but some of whom we met on our way through Vanuatu itself. We had a great group of boats with families onboard ('kid boats'), broadly sailing the same direction and joining up periodically for various adventures together. Not only that, we also crossed paths with other boats sailing back south as we were heading north, in particular we were delighted to spend time with Bruce and Alene on Migration, even though our stay at the festival in Wala was unfortunately cut short by the anchorage difficulties.

It's not without some trepidation that we find ourselves sailing north out of Vanuatu with our friends on Jadamama, bound 'up and over' Papua New Guinea (PNG). With everyone else heading back south to the abundant supplies and services available in New Zealand and Australia, we are heading further away from chandleries, boatyards, supermarkets and any other support or assistance. We really have to be self-sufficient now, it doesn't get much more remote than this.
