Every village seems to have its own fishing technique. 

## Traditional fishing in Port Resolution
On arrival in Tanna we were surprised on our first morning to be surrounded by local fishing canoes. There were dozens of them all over the bay, setting nets all around the anchorage between and around our boats. Sometimes they'd be actively fishing, splashing the water and frantically paddling around trying to encircle fish with their nets, and other times they'd be passive just leaving the nets out and returning ashore for a few hours. On our first morning we were asked by one to charge his phone whilst he was away, he'd be back later to pick it up. By mid-afternoon we were beginning to wonder if he'd actually come back, but of course he did. Such trusting and friendly people to us, complete strangers in their waters. What a contrast in technology too, mobiles are everywhere even though they don't have rope to tie up their pigs (rope is a commonly requested trading item for this purpose) and the wealtheir roofing material for their homes is corrugated iron, as it lasts longer than woven coconut leaves.

## High speed donuts in Tutuba
This paddling technique was in stark contrast to what we witnessed in Tutuba. Here they have some snorkels and masks as well as access to a lot more petrol, being only a few miles out from (the relatively large for Vanuatu town) Luganville. This was a different experience altogether. A couple of guys would be snorkelling, looking at the schools of fish underwater and directing the guys in their canoes laying out nets. Then they'd start splashing and gesturing with their hands (all the while with their heads down, watching the fish) - these were signals to the guys in their pangas (larger fibreglass boats with ~40HP outboards), to get in on the action. Much to our astonishment they'd start doing high speed donuts, often right between our anchored yachts, but occassionally directly around one of us. Quite extraordinary, you couldn't help but feel we were right in their way, but in talking with them they seemed perfectly happy with us there and apparently enjoyed working their magic around us (they seemed far too happy for it to be any kind of intimidation technique). Not exactly the calmest anchorage with all this going on, but nevertheless highly entertaining to witness close up! We think they were using their engines to drive the fish into their nets, and I guess it must work to justify the cost of all that petrol even though we didn't witness heavily laden nets at the end of it all. We've all seen the BBC documentaries of whales bubblenet feeding, this is probably a similar plan to encircle fish.

## Our Own Fishing Experience

Our own fishing experience in Vanuatu started off very much in keeping with our experience in New Caledonia - nothing to show for our hours of trolling! Having been happily successful fishing all around New Zealand, we were beginning to wonder whether we'd ever catch something again since leaving. The change came when we were sailing with Paul up to Port Vila. We had a big strike whilst sailing at ~8 knots with the gennaker up. With the reel pushed up to max drag we simply could not stop the line going out, so set about trying to roll away the gennaker as quickly as possible. Even with Paul onboard to help we only managed to half roll it before the reel ran out of line completely and it just snapped off the spindle - losing everything; lure, leader, snap swivel and a few hundred metres of line. I was pretty sad to say the least, losing a lure here or there is annoying, but losing the whole line quickly gets expensive! Whatever it was we had on the end was way too big. Having beefed up all our gear it seems we have solved the problem we used to have, of breaking something when we catch too big a fish - this time it all held together, but instead we ended up losing the whole lot - so maybe we were better off with undersized hooks and leaders so something at the fish end fails first?!

Nevertheless we didn't give up, and not long later on passage from Ambrym to Wala (a small island off Malekula) we caught and landed our biggest ever fish. We managed to slow the boat enough this time to arrest the line and at least hold it stationary whilst we dealt with the sails. Bearing away to dead downwind is our first immediate reaction (assuming the wind is behind the beam), following which we drop the jib (a little more 'drop it in a heap' than 'careful textbook flake' on the trampolene), before reefing the main. We've got quicker now at this manoeuvre, on a dead downwind course we can reef the main with both Sophie and I at the mast - Sophie eases the halyard whilst I wind in each reef, with the children minding the autopilot and the fish. Generally we drop all the way down to third reef which should get our boatspeed down to around 4 knots. This is still fast enough to make it hard work to reel in a big fish, but at least it seems to be possible. From there our luck is in the lap of the gods, Zipper's hulls are very narrow at the stern so getting a fish onboard is a combination of as much luck as it is experience and judgement. Any or all of a large laundry-style flexible bucket, net, gaff and willpower are deployed depending on the day and the fish. This time it was clear that the fish was big enough that gaff and willpower were the only options, we had to simply drag it up the steps onto the deck, resulting in a mess of blood flying everywhere, but we landed it. Wahoooooooooooo!! I struggled to lift it high enough to weigh on our scales, but we reckon this one was 18kg, what a beautiful fish. Unfortunately in the fight to get the fish out of the water it broke the tip off the rod - which seems to be the going rate for landing a big wahoo; our friends on Jadamama recently suffered exactly the same fate.

I dragged the fish all the way forward and filleted it up on the foredeck, the first time our fish table (engine room hatch) hasn't been big enough. Arriving into Wala that afternoon, ahead of the festival, there were several other yachts in the anchorage we were able to share it with - the fish fed us for three days and at least 5 other yachts for at least one if not two evenings.

![Wahoo fillets]({{ site.baseurl }}/assets/images/gallery/2026-09/wahoo-fillets.webp)

We haven't found any moderately sized fish in Vanuatu, we wonder if over-fishing basically means there aren't any. In our experience you either get nothing (most of the time) or you get a strike from a whopper that's either too big to handle or right at the limit of what we can land. Something a bit more middling in size would be welcome, please!
