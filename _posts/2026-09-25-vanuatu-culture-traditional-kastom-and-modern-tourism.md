Vanuatu is a treasure trove of indiginous cultural experiences. While kastom, the islands' foundational way of life including spiritual beliefs, are coming under increased pressure with integration into Western (and more recently, Eastern, particularly Chinese) developments, technology and other global influences, many villages and communities are striving to use festivals and tourism to keep their traditions active. Despite the missionaries' best efforts, converting over 90% of the population to Christianity, traditional spiritual beliefs have not been lost, rather Christianity has merged in alongside them creating a complex culture where both are practiced.

Parts of Vanuatu, around Port Vila in particular, have become popular tourist destinations and as such lay on modern shows and activities for tourists to enjoy (and spend money on!). One such location is the Beach Bar outside Port Vila where we attended an excellent circus performance one week as well as a truly spectacular fireshow the next. Uncle Wilf (thanks Paul!) extremely generously took care of the 'spend money' part for us, so we could simply enjoy the spectacle. On landing on the beach ahead of the fireshow we were met by Tom, the Australian owner who was extremely friendly and welcoming. He suggested that inflatables and petrol engines don't mix well with fire, so we might want to move the dinghy all the way over to the pier. There I was thinking we were surely far enough away and wondering why health and safety was going over the top here, having been completely absent everywhere else including on the rim of an active volcano!

> inflatables and petrol engines don't mix well with fire

Well, I got my answer during the show; it was a good job we did move the dingy as the beach certainly was covered in fire as you can see from the photos.

## Sand drawing

We learned a lot about language and communication visiting the Vanuatu Cultural Centre in Port Vila. We picked up some basic phrases in Bislama (Vanuatu's development of pidgin English), and understand that although there were more than 1,000 languages across the islands in the past, the ~100 that remain still make it the highest number of languages per head of anywhere in the world. The museum was small, but fascinating as we (as a large group of family cruisers visiting together) were treated to a guided tour including a display of traditional sand drawing and music played on various traditional instruments.

Our guide demonstrated elaborate sand drawings that he was able to produce in less than 5 minutes each. He started each by shaking the sand to form a flat, even, surface on the board (of course traditionally this would simply be done on the beach) and then drew a grid. This forms the structure on which the drawing is built up. He then placed his index finger in the middle of the grid and simply started to move it in graceful swirls around the board. He moved it slowly but deliberately at a constant speed, not lifting it once, until the drawing was complete. Amazing memory and level of skill to end up with a perfectly symmetrical and flowing drawing!

## Wala festival

After much deliberation we decided to attend the festival in Wala, a small island off Malekula. The anchorage was challenging to say the least, and sadly we had to leave in the middle of the night between the two days of the festival, but we did thoroughly enjoy attending the first day. The first day of the festival was held on the mainland, a bit of a walk from the beach. We shared this walk with a huge delegation of children from local schools, many of whom absolutely mobbed Angus - they all wanted to be his best friend, fighting and pushing each other to be the one walking with their arm around him. This was a pretty overwhelming experience! Isla escaped equivalent attention by virtue of walking faster at the front of the group, ahead of the school groups.

## Gaua water music

Who knew it is possible to make music with your bare hands and water? Ok, yes, we can all make _noise_ (and we did when it got to our turn), but some very talented ladies perform this particular art form in groups and actually make _music_. The chief at Gaua arranged a performance for just our two boats. We stood on the beach to watch as 8 ladies with beautiful flower headdresses waded into the water waist deep, lined up, and played their songs for us.

The videos we took don't quite do the sound justice, in particular the underlying base note sounds solid and hollow enough that if you closed your eyes it sounded indistinguishable from a wooden drum. It is quite extraordinary the sounds, and variety of sounds, that they are able to make. We were all (even those suffering from the norovirus, above) moved by what we heard, it really was a special experience to witness.

We asked Samantha, the leader, (daughter of the Chief and head mistress at the school) if she could show us how so we could try ourselves, and she was delighted to. First though, she took us to the fresh water pool behind the beach - as incompetent beginners I think she recognised we had no chance starting off in salt water! As it turned out we didn't really get anywhere anyway - try as we might to grab the water in the right ways we just couldn't recreate the low echo boom sounds that Samantha conjurs up at will. Talk about making something look easy! To be fair to Isla, she did start to make some of the right sounds by the end. However for the rest of us we were soaked from splashing water everywhere and although the sounds we were after proved elusive we absolutely loved the experience and were in hysterics.

> Huge respect to the ladies performing, they were genuinely amazing.

## Kava

I couldn't write a piece on Vanuatu kastom without mentioning kava. Where we head to the pub and have a beer, the Vanuatans (well, the men) go to the village nakamal (a clearing normally under a banyon tree where men get together to discuss local issues, global issues and generally anything else) and drink kava. The difference is they do this at 4pm, however they do also tend to return home a lot earlier than we would from the pub, so perhaps that's OK. The nakamal is a formal setting where women are still not allowed. However this is not to say that women don't drink kava, these days informal kava bars where anyone can go for a drink are common. In our experience it can be hard to leave a kava bar as visitors are welcomed in and agreeing to 'just one' means you'll be hard pressed to get out before you've had a few and your mouth is well and truly numbed. Vanuatans are adament their kava is superior in quality to that in Fiji, having not made it to Fiji after the weather sent us to [New Caledonia]({{ site.baseurl }}{% post_url 2026-06-09-the-passage-north %}) instead, we'll have to take their word for it.
